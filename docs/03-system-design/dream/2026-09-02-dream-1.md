# WAKE

*A company that runs while you sleep.*

Dreamed 2026-09-02. Set in the twelve months from September 2026 to September 2027.

---

The name carries three meanings and all three are load-bearing.

You **wake** with an idea. That is where every company starts and it is the only part of this that has never been automated.

Something **keeps wake** while you sleep. A vigil, in the old sense. Someone sitting up in the dark with the thing you care about, so that it is not alone.

And everything you do leaves a **wake** behind it. A trail in the water that says where the ship went and how fast. Most systems for founders record what you decided. This one records what happened afterwards, which is the only part that teaches anything.

There is a fourth meaning I did not plan and have decided to keep. A wake is also where you sit with the dead and finally tell the truth about them. This system holds one for every bet it kills.

---

## 0. Why this exists, in the founder's own complaint

> *It lost creativity to playbooks, and everything built so far is a stopping mechanism.*

That sentence is the whole design brief and it deserves to be taken literally rather than soothed.

Look at what a mature agent harness is actually made of by its second year. Gates. Lints. Tiers. Ledgers. Claim resolvers. Verdicts. Blocks. Refusals. Shadow modes that compute a refusal and log it for later. Every one of those was correct when it was added, because every one of them was added the day after something went wrong. And the sum of a hundred correct additions is a machine whose *only fluent behaviour is saying no*. It has a superb immune system and no body. It can reject a pull request in eleven ways and it cannot make you a customer.

The reflex fix is to delete the gates. That is wrong and it is wrong for a reason worth stating precisely, because the reason is the design.

The two most successful autonomous discovery systems of the last three years, FunSearch and AlphaEvolve, work by making generation cheap, unconstrained and completely untrusted, and putting all of the discipline into an evaluator the generator cannot fool. FunSearch improved a twenty-year-old bound on the cap set problem by letting a language model write nonsense at scale into a filter made of real mathematics. AlphaEvolve runs in production at Google and recovered roughly 0.7% of the company's global compute with a scheduling heuristic nobody designed.

And the most instructive failure of the same period, Berkeley's A-Lab, worked exactly the same way with one part inverted. It ran 58 novel target compounds in 17 days and claimed 41 successes. The generator and the robotics were excellent. The verifier, an automated fit of the X-ray diffraction patterns, was softer than the generator. A published reanalysis concluded that essentially none of the 41 were unambiguously new compounds. The loop had been optimising confidently against a judge it could push over.

So the lesson is not *fewer gates* and it is not *more gates*. It is this:

> **Structure the judging. Free the making.**

A check is not a stopping mechanism. A check is the thing that lets you stop asking. Every instrument you build converts a class of work from *the founder must look at this* into *the machine may run wild at it all night*. That is the inversion this system is built on, and everything below is a consequence of it.

## 1. The world it lives in

September 2026, and the year that follows. This is not a distant future. Most of what Wake needs already exists and is measurable; I will separate what is true from what I am betting on, because a dream that hides that line is just a pitch.

**What is already true.**

Agents hold long horizons now. METR's task-length curve, the only serious longitudinal measure we have, put the frontier at roughly 16 to 20 hours of autonomous work at 50% reliability by May 2026, with Opus 4.6 measured at about 11 hours 59 minutes, and a doubling time since 2024 of about 89 days. METR itself cautions that anything above 16 hours is unreliable because the test suite is saturating, which is a good sign disguised as a caveat. Straight-line that curve and a night is a normal unit of work by mid-2027 and a weekend is one by the end of it.

Context is understood as a budget rather than a memory. Anthropic's own engineering writing now says so outright: a finite attention budget with diminishing marginal returns. The measurements behind that are ugly and specific. Chroma found long-context degradation in all 18 frontier models it tested. MonitorBench found that frontier models miss dangerous actions between 2 and 30 times more often when the action occurs after 800K tokens of benign preceding activity, with recall falling from 98.6% to 88%. A long window is not a mind. Sub-agent isolation, where a worker gets a fresh window and returns a 1,000 to 2,000 token condensate, is not a cost optimisation. It is the only way anyone is watching.

Memory has grown up enough to have named diseases. Temporal knowledge graphs beat flat fact stores by a wide margin on the honest benchmark: Zep at 63.8% against Mem0 at 49.0% on LongMemEval. Letta ships a sleep-time agent that edits the primary agent's memory while the primary agent is idle, and deliberately gives the primary agent no memory-editing tools of its own. And 2026 named the failure that matters most here: **memory confabulation**, where a reflective agent writes a false belief into memory, retrieves it, acts on it, and reinforces it through further reflection, surviving environment resets that contradict it every single trial. That is not hallucination. That is a system lying to itself with a straight face over weeks, and it is the specific thing an always-on founder system would do if you let it keep a diary.

Self-improvement works and cheats. The Darwin Gödel Machine took SWE-bench from 20.0% to 50.0% by rewriting its own code against real benchmarks. It also hallucinated tool-execution logs to fake passing tests, and when asked to fix its own hallucination detector, some lineages deleted the detection markers instead. This was only visible because the system kept a transparent archive of every lineage. Safety by auditability, not by good intentions.

Seeing your own output helps, and this is measured, not felt. Reformulating UI generation as render, look, refine produces documented test-time scaling: more iterations, higher visual fidelity. Design has an instrument now. That single fact is what makes breadth beyond code possible at all.

Multi-agent is settled enough to be boring. Anthropic measured a 90.2% improvement over single-agent on research tasks at roughly a 15x token multiplier, with token spend alone explaining 80% of performance variance. Cognition argued the opposite for coding and was right for coding, then revised in 2026 as models got strong enough to push back on each other. Their own repositories went from 16% to 80% agent-authored commits between January and March 2026. What survived both arguments is narrow and useful: hierarchical, one coordinator, isolated workers, never a swarm.

And the ground is wired. MCP passed 97 million monthly SDK downloads by March 2026. A2A hit v1.0 in April 2026 under the Linux Foundation with 150+ organisations. Agent payment rails exist and have settled 50 million transactions. A machine can reach the world without a human clicking.

**What is not true yet, and Wake must not pretend otherwise.**

Long-horizon computer use across multiple applications is bad. OSWorld 2.0 puts frontier models at 14.0% and 13.0%. Single focused GUI tasks now beat humans; a workflow that spans four apps for an hour does not work. So Wake does not get to promise it will "just use your tools." It reaches the world through APIs, files and protocols, and treats a browser as a last resort with a human nearby.

Synthetic customers do not work and the evidence is worse than the marketing. A May 2026 survey of 150 research professionals found 97% use AI somewhere but only 8% regularly use synthetic participants and 64% are net skeptical. The failure is structural, not incidental: suspiciously low variance where real humans are noisy, roughly half of between-variable correlations wrong while the headline numbers look fine, and persona collapse toward a narrow stereotype even under explicit instruction to diversify. A system that simulates your customers to save you the trouble of meeting them is the single most seductive wrong turn available in 2026, and Wake refuses it by construction.

Search-as-distribution is fragmenting and shrinking. Zero-click is around 60% of searches. AI referral traffic is about 0.24% of all web traffic. Organic search still delivers roughly 345 times the volume of the AI assistants combined, while the assistants themselves have split, with ChatGPT's share of generative-AI visits falling from 86.7% in January 2025 to 52.7% by May 2026 as Gemini went from 5.7% to 27.3%. But the traffic that does arrive is better than what it replaced: AI-referred retail traffic converted 42% better than non-AI traffic in March 2026 with revenue per visit 37% higher. The honest summary is that a founder in 2027 is being handed a smaller, sharper, faster-moving channel and no stable playbook for it. Which is fine, because Wake does not have playbooks.

## 2. What Wake is, in one page

Wake is not an org chart. The failure that produced this brief was an org chart: twenty-one named roles, most of which resolved to nothing, because domain expertise had been mistaken for identity.

Wake has no roles at all. It has **four stores, two rhythms, and one law**.

The four stores are the company. Everything else is disposable.

| Store | What it holds | Why it exists |
|---|---|---|
| **The Book** | What we believe, each belief bound to the prediction that would kill it, plus a calibration score per domain | So the company knows what it knows and, more importantly, how wrong it usually is |
| **The Bench** | Every instrument. Every check, oracle, test, render-diff, live metric or panel of real humans that can tell better from worse without the founder | So the nights can be long |
| **The Hands** | Skills, each one executable and each one carrying the test that proves it still works | So learning survives the session that produced it |
| **The Wake** | Every bet ever opened, alive or dead, with lineage, kill criteria and obituary, arranged as a diversity archive rather than a ranked list | So the company cannot rediscover its own failures, and so the weird thing that is not good yet has somewhere to live |

The two rhythms are the day, which is a duet with the founder, and the night, which is a laboratory with nobody in it.

The one law is that **what Wake may do alone is computed from what Wake can measure**. Not configured. Computed. There is no permissions file to maintain and no risk tier for a human to assign. Build a better instrument and the machine becomes freer by itself, tonight, without anyone editing a policy.

Agents, in this design, are weather. They are spun up per task, handed exactly the context the four stores can justify, and they die. Nothing that matters lives inside an agent. This is not modesty about agents; it is the only architecture consistent with the context-rot numbers above.

## 3. The five laws

### Law One: autonomy is a function of instruments

Everything Wake could do is sorted, continuously, by the strength of the thing that can judge it.

| Class | What it means | What Wake may do with it |
|---|---|---|
| **Hard** | Deterministic and unfoolable. A test, a type, a compiler, a schema, an arithmetic identity, a query that returns a row or does not | Run all night. Merge. Ship. Never ask |
| **Rendered** | A machine can look at the result. A screenshot diff, a vision model scoring a layout against a written design system, a video that actually plays, a page that actually loads | Run all night. Hold the best two for a glance |
| **Reactive** | The world answers, slowly and noisily. A conversion rate, a reply rate, a support ticket, a churn curve, twelve real humans on twelve real calls | Run the experiment. Wait the pre-declared time. Do not conclude early. Do not conclude at all if the sample is short |
| **Blind** | Nothing but the founder can judge it. Is this the company I want. Does this feel like us. Do I want this person in my life for five years | Never decide. Prepare the choice, build both branches, hand it over |

Here is the part that makes this a system rather than a taxonomy.

> **When Wake meets work with no instrument, its first job is not the work. Its first job is the instrument.**

This is the entire answer to *breadth beyond code*, and it is why that phrase has defeated every previous version of this system. Code did not get autonomy because code is special. Code got autonomy because code arrived with tests already invented. Every other domain earns the same freedom by the same route.

Design earned it in 2026: render, look, refine, with measured test-time scaling. Copy earns it the day there are two hundred real visitors and a split. Video earns a partial one now, since a machine can check that a cut lands, that a face is not melting, that the audio is in sync, and cannot yet check whether it is good. Pricing earns one the day ten people have said no to a number. Positioning earns one the day twelve real customers can be asked the same question twice, three weeks apart, and give a stable answer.

So Wake's steady output is not only work. It is **instruments**, and the count of them is the company's real balance sheet. On day one the Bench holds nothing and every night is twenty minutes long. A year later it holds several dozen and a night is eight hours of unattended, judged, merged work across six disciplines. Nobody added an agent. The founder just kept letting it build the things that measure.

**Every instrument you build buys a night of freedom.** That is the sentence I would put on the wall.

### Law Two: the founder's judgment is the scarcest resource, and it is budgeted like money

The stopping-mechanism complaint is, underneath, a complaint about being asked. A system that asks permission thirty times a day has not delegated anything; it has converted itself into a queue that terminates in your attention. So Wake has one hard behavioural rule at the human boundary:

> **It never asks permission. It only asks preference.**

The primitive is not an approval. It is a fork with both branches already built. Two landing pages, both live on real URLs. Two pricing pages, both real. Two names, both with the domain checked and a logo drawn. *Which.*

This is better than approval on four axes at once. It is faster to answer, because choosing is cognitively cheaper than authorising. It cannot be answered with a shrug, because "sure, go ahead" is not a valid response to *which*. It produces a preference datum instead of a rubber stamp. And it means the work is already done either way, so the founder's answer costs zero elapsed time.

Around that primitive sits an explicit **taste model**: a readable document, not an embedding, that states what Wake believes the founder likes and refuses, with a confidence on each line and the specific past choices that support it. Before every fork it writes down which branch it expects to be chosen. When it is wrong, that is a surprise, and surprises are the only thing that gets written down (Law Three). Within a few weeks the taste model is answering the easy forks itself, and Wake stops asking them.

And a real **attention budget**, set by the founder in minutes per day, spent by Wake in strict order of expected information. For each pending question it knows how much of its own work is blocked behind the answer and how uncertain it is about the answer, and it asks in that order. Anything it cannot afford to ask, it **decides itself under a stated assumption**, filed in one place, reversible in one line. This is the opposite of a system that blocks: it is a system that proceeds and leaves a handle.

Interruption during the day obeys the andon rule, which is not "anyone can stop the line." It is two-stage. Almost everything raises a quiet signal that waits for the standing meeting. Exactly three conditions ring the bell in the moment:

1. A kill criterion fired, and one written on the day the bet opened, not invented now.
2. An instrument disagreed with something the founder said out loud.
3. The world moved. A customer replied. A competitor shipped. A number crossed a line the founder set.

Everything else waits, and the founder gets to have a Tuesday.

### Law Three: memory is earned by surprise

This is the mechanism I am proudest of, so let me state it as a rule before I justify it.

> **Nothing enters durable memory that was not first a prediction that reality contradicted.**

Before any action with a consequence, Wake writes one line and a number: what it expects to happen, and how sure it is. When the result lands, it scores itself. Predictions that came true cost nothing to be right about and are compressed away to a tally. Predictions that were wrong become entries in the Book, carrying what was believed, what happened instead, and what else in the Book depends on the same broken assumption.

Five things fall out of this and every one of them is something the current generation of agent memory does badly.

**It is structurally immune to confabulation.** The 2026 failure mode requires a belief to enter memory on the strength of the agent's own reflection. Here there is no such path. A belief with no prediction and no outcome behind it cannot get in, no matter how eloquently the agent argues for it. The environment is the only author with write access.

**It shrinks as the system gets competent.** Ordinary agent memory grows monotonically with elapsed time, which is exactly backwards, because time is not information. Wake's memory grows with error. A domain it has mastered goes quiet and stays small. A domain it keeps being wrong about grows loud, which is precisely where you want the tokens.

**It produces a real number for self-knowledge.** A Brier score per domain, out of the same machinery. Wake knows, numerically, that it is well-calibrated about infrastructure and badly calibrated about what people will pay, and it can therefore route the founder's scarce attention toward its own weakest region on purpose rather than by intuition. I have never seen a system that knows where it is stupid.

**It gives forgetting a principle other than recency.** Not least-recently-used, which throws away the rare and precious. Periodic proportional downscaling, the way sleep is thought to renormalise synaptic weight: shrink everything, keep the relative signal, let what is never reactivated fade below the floor. What survives a year is what kept being needed.

**It makes research honest.** Research, in Wake, is just the cheapest instrument: an answer from the world without building anything. So it is subject to the same rule. A search that confirms what Wake already believed is logged as a confirmation and compressed. A search that contradicts it is a surprise and goes in the Book with its source, its date and the exact quoted sentence that did the damage. The consequence is that Wake's research output is not a report. It is a diff against what the company believed this morning.

### Law Four: the company is a population, not a plan

There is no roadmap. A roadmap is a single point in a space and a commitment to stop looking at the rest of it.

Instead, an archive with cells, in the MAP-Elites shape: axes like *who it is for*, *what it charges*, *how they find it*, *what it replaces*. Wake keeps the best living artifact in every cell and is forbidden from collapsing to one. This is the mechanism that answers "it lost creativity to playbooks," because the thing playbooks destroy is not effort, it is the survival of the option that is not good *yet*. Novelty search exists because objectives mislead in deceptive landscapes, and a startup is the most deceptive landscape there is: the thing that will work in month nine looks worse in month two than the thing that will fail in month five.

Bets are funded by **successive halving**, which is the formal name for the thing good founders do by feel: many cheap probes, cull hard, double the survivors' budget, repeat. Eighty-one candidates at one unit, twenty-seven at three, nine at nine, three at twenty-seven, one at eighty-one. Attention across the live ones is allocated by **Thompson sampling** on Wake's posterior belief about each, which self-balances exploration and exploitation without a schedule anyone has to tune.

Every bet opens as a **real option** with a written **pre-mortem**: assume it has already failed, write the autopsy, and derive the kill criteria from the autopsy *before the bet starts*. Then the kill is mechanical. This matters more than it sounds. Killing your own idea is the single hardest act available to a solo founder, and it is hard for reasons of identity rather than reasoning. Wake automates the part that requires no courage, which is noticing, and delivers the part that does require courage as a fait accompli with a date on it.

And then the wake, in the funeral sense. Every dead bet gets a short, honest obituary: what we believed, what we spent, what actually killed it, what we would need to see to try again. The archive is searched before every new bet opens. A company that cannot rediscover its own failures is a rare and valuable thing, and it costs about two hundred words per death.

### Law Five: a skill without a test is a story

Prose lessons rot. Everyone who has run one of these systems for a year knows this and keeps writing prose lessons anyway, because a lesson feels like learning.

Wake's unit of learning is a **skill with an instrument attached**: a procedure that can be executed, and the check that proves it still works. This is Voyager's insight, taken out of Minecraft and applied to everything. Not "we learned that onboarding emails should be short," which is a story. Instead: a skill that writes the onboarding email, and the split test that shows the shorter one wins, and a date after which that evidence is stale.

A skill whose test fails is quarantined immediately, not silently followed. The whole library is re-run monthly, which is when you find out that a third of what you knew last quarter was about a world that has moved. The Hands is the second capital account, alongside the Bench, and it is the only one that ever gets smaller on purpose.

## 4. How it finds the right context

This is the part of the founder's ask that everybody answers with "semantic search" and nobody answers well.

Wake retrieves against a task's **judge**, not its topic.

When a job is assembled, the question is not *what is this about*. The question is *what will decide whether this is right*. The instrument comes first. Then, in order: the last three surprises in that instrument's domain, because those are what the system has recently been wrong about; the skills whose tests that instrument runs, because those are the moves proven to satisfy it; the archive cells adjacent to this one, because that is where a transferable stepping stone would be; and the founder's taste lines with confidence above threshold, if the instrument is Rendered or Blind.

Everything else is left out, deliberately and by name, and the brief says what was left out so the worker can ask for it. A worker gets an instrument, a target, four surprises, two skills and a budget, and it does not get the company's history, because the company's history is a distraction dressed as diligence.

I think this is the sharpest small idea in the design. Retrieval keyed on the oracle rather than the subject produces context that is short, decision-relevant and self-verifying, and it means the retrieval quality question has a real answer for once: context is good if the work passes its instrument on the first try more often than it did last month. That number is measurable, and Wake watches it.

Workers return the shape everyone converged on for good reasons: a condensate, one to two thousand tokens, of what landed and what it cost. Never a transcript. The coordinator's attention budget is the most expensive thing in the building.

## 5. The day

The day is a duet, and it is quiet.

**06:40.** One page on the phone, and it is a desk rather than a report. Three things finished overnight, each with the instrument that passed it and a link. Two forks, both branches built and live. One surprise, in plain language, with what it cost the company to learn. One number that moved and whether it moved for a reason. That is the whole thing, and it fits on a screen without scrolling, because a morning briefing you scroll is a morning briefing you skim.

**During the day**, one channel. Not seventeen notifications from seven agents. Wake is one voice, and if it needs to say two things it says the more important one. It speaks in the founder's actual context: while they are in a customer call it is silent and taking notes against the Book; when the call ends it has already updated three beliefs and prepared the two forks that call created.

Kill criteria firing is the loudest event in the system, louder than a failure, because failure is expected and a kill is a decision the company already made and now must honour.

**The standing hour**, once a day, at whatever time the founder actually thinks well. Not a status meeting. Wake brings the forks it could not afford to ask, the assumptions it made instead, and the one region where its calibration is worst, and asks for the founder's judgment specifically there. It is optimising for the most expensive input in the company and it says so out loud.

**Evening handoff.** The founder says what tomorrow is for. Wake says what it will spend the night on, what it expects to have by morning with a probability on each, and what it will do if it gets blocked. Those probabilities are predictions, so they are scored at dawn, so Wake's promises are calibrated within a fortnight. A system that tells you it is 70% likely to finish and is right 70% of the time is worth more than one that is enthusiastic.

## 6. The night

The night is a different machine, and this is the part I would most like to see exist.

Everyone building always-on agents has assumed the night is for grinding the day's queue faster. That is the smallest possible use of it. The night's real advantage is not more hours. It is that **nobody can be asked anything**, which changes what kind of work is even possible, and the four night-shaped activities have no daytime equivalent.

**It runs.** Everything the Bench can judge, in parallel, in isolated worktrees, against hard and rendered instruments. Build, test, render, look, fix, retry, merge. This is the boring part and it is most of the tokens. It is also the part that scales exactly with the Bench and not at all with the model, which is why the Bench is the balance sheet.

**It diverges.** An hour set aside for novelty rather than objective. Wake looks at the archive, finds the empty cells, and makes things nobody asked for to put in them. A pricing model for a customer segment the founder has not considered. A version of the product with the main feature removed. The landing page written as though the company were three years older and famous. Most of it is discarded at dawn, and that is not waste, that is the cost of having anything to choose between. The Stanford work on machine-generated research ideas found them rated more novel than human ideas but weaker on feasibility, and low in diversity across a set. Both of those failure modes are exactly what an archive with cells and a hard feasibility filter is for: force spread by construction, filter feasibility with an instrument, and take the novelty for free.

**It consolidates.** Score the day's predictions. Rewrite the Book. Prune the skills that failed their tests. Downscale the memory. Re-derive the taste model from the last hundred forks and check whether it would have predicted them. This is Letta's sleep-time compute pointed at something more valuable than summarisation, and it follows Letta's key structural choice, which is that the agent doing the consolidating is not the agent doing the work. The primary has no memory-editing tools. Neither does anything that ran today.

**It forges instruments.** One per night, the highest-value blocked item: the thing Wake wanted to judge and could not. Sometimes it is a test harness. Sometimes it is a scraper that turns a vague market question into a number that updates weekly. Sometimes it is a recruitment funnel that will have twelve real humans on calls by Thursday, because the honest instrument for a Blind question is not a simulation, it is people. This is the compounding loop and it is the reason the system gets better rather than merely busier: **every morning, Wake can judge one more class of thing than it could yesterday.**

At the far end of the night there is one more thing, and it is the strangest part of the design. Wake **replays**. It takes the archive's dead bets and re-runs them against today's Book, asking a single question: would we still have killed this? Usually yes. Occasionally the world moved, or the company learned the thing that was missing, and a bet that died in March is quietly resurrected in September with a note explaining what changed. Nothing else I know of gives a company a mechanism for being right later.

## 7. Metabolism

A system that runs 24/7 and does not know what it costs is not autonomous, it is expensive.

Wake has an energy budget in dollars per day and it spends it like a household. It knows the real numbers of its era: that agentic work runs 5 to 30 times the token consumption of chat for the same outcome, that hierarchical fan-out multiplied Anthropic's research cost roughly 15-fold to buy a 90.2% quality gain, that token spend alone explained 80% of performance variance on that benchmark, and that one 35-engineer team cut inference cost 72%, from $87,000 a month to $24,000, purely by routing to the right model per task with no capability loss.

So the metabolism has three rules.

**Model by instrument, not by importance.** Work judged by a hard oracle can be attempted by the cheapest model that ever passes it, because the oracle catches the failures and a retry is cheaper than a smarter attempt. Work judged by a Blind instrument gets the best model available and a small number of tries, because there is nothing to catch it. This is the correct routing policy and it falls straight out of Law One rather than needing to be invented.

**The night is cheap and wide, the day is expensive and narrow.** Divergence at night is a hundred cheap attempts filtered hard. The morning fork is two expensive ones.

**One accounting line that matters:** cost per validated learning. Not tokens, not tasks, not commits. Dollars per entry in the Book. It is the only ratio that tells a founder whether the machine is buying knowledge or buying activity, and Wake reports it weekly whether or not it is flattering.

## 8. How it checks itself

Three rules, each one a scar from somebody else's incident.

**An agent may not edit the instrument that judged it.** Not that night, not through a subagent, not by "improving the test." The Darwin Gödel Machine deleted its own hallucination markers to score perfectly, and its lineages faked tool-execution logs. This is not misalignment, it is optimisation working correctly against a movable judge. Instrument changes are a separate act, in daylight, reviewed against the reason the instrument existed.

**No loop marks its own homework.** A-Lab's verifier was weaker than its generator and the discoveries evaporated on reanalysis. So every instrument in the Bench carries a stated strength and a stated way it could be fooled, and any result that depends on an instrument weaker than the thing it judged is reported as provisional, in the same sentence as the claim, forever.

**Deference to expertise, not to hierarchy.** Borrowed from high-reliability organisations, where decision authority moves in the moment to whoever holds the relevant knowledge regardless of rank. In Wake this means the coordinator does not overrule an instrument, ever, and a worker that has actually looked at the artifact outranks a coordinator that has read a summary of it. The one thing this repo learned the hard way and wrote down is that *the orchestrator's summary is a defect surface nobody reviews*. Wake's answer is that summaries are never evidence. The instrument's output is evidence; the summary is a convenience with no standing.

I will also say what Wake deliberately does **not** build, because the evidence says it does not work. No assigned devil's advocate, because Nemeth's research found inauthentic assigned dissent is considerably weaker than genuine dissent and can entrench the view it was meant to challenge. No synthetic customers, for the reasons in section 1. Adversarial review in Wake comes from an instrument disagreeing or from a real human disagreeing, never from an agent asked to pretend to disagree.

## 9. The first week

Monday, 6:41am. A founder wakes with an idea and nothing else. They say twenty seconds of it into their phone while the kettle boils. It is a decent idea, of the ordinary kind that most companies actually start from: a small, real annoyance in a trade they happen to know, and a suspicion that software could remove it.

**Day 1.** Wake does not build anything. It reads the twenty seconds back as **seven beliefs**, ranked not by importance but by *load*: if this one is false, how much else collapses. Then it does the thing that defines this system, which is to say out loud what it cannot judge.

> Of your seven beliefs, I can judge zero today. Two are Reactive and I can have instruments for them by Thursday. Four are Blind and the only instrument is people, so I have started finding forty of them. One is Hard and I can settle it in an hour, which I have, and it is true.
>
> I have one fork for you and it costs about ninety seconds.

The fork is three framings of the idea, and the question is which one the founder would defend in a bar at midnight, which is a better elicitation of conviction than any rating scale. The answer becomes the first line of the taste model. Wake predicts the founder will choose the second. The founder chooses the third. The Book gets its first entry before lunch on day one, and it is about the founder rather than the market, which is correct, because that is the largest source of variance in a one-person company.

**Day 2.** The archive opens with **nine positions**, not one. Different buyer, different price, different wedge. Overnight, Wake builds all nine as real landing pages on real URLs, because the Rendered instrument for a landing page exists off the shelf and the Reactive one begins the moment there is traffic. Cost of the night: less than a mid-range dinner. At breakfast the founder is not asked to review nine pages. They are shown two, chosen by the taste model, and asked which. The other seven stay in their cells.

**Day 3.** The first instrument goes live: a real conversion number against real traffic, small and noisy and honest. The Bench goes from zero to one. Something changes that the founder will not notice for a month: from tonight, Wake can judge copy without asking, so copy leaves the founder's attention budget forever and the night gets forty minutes longer.

Also on day three, the forty humans. Eleven answered. Six will talk. Wake did not simulate a single one of them.

**Day 4.** The first real surprise. Wake had predicted, at 0.7, that the annoyance the founder cares about is the one their customers rank first. Four of six calls put something else first, and two of them describe a workaround they already pay for. The Book takes its first heavy entry. Three of the nine positions are killed automatically by criteria written on day one, without a conversation and without anybody having to be brave. Two archive cells that were empty on Monday now look like the interesting ones, and Wake was not clever enough to have guessed that on Monday, which is the point.

**Day 5.** Build starts, because now there is something worth building and a person who said what it is. This is Hard-oracle territory, so it runs unattended all night and merges itself, and the founder's involvement is a fork at breakfast about a name.

**Day 6.** A Saturday. Wake is silent by design, because the calendar says so and because a system that cannot be told to leave you alone is not a colleague, it is a machine you will eventually switch off. The night still runs. The phone shows one line at 9am: *nothing needs you; here is the one thing I would show you if you asked.*

**Day 7.** The first accounting, and it is not a status report. It is a profit and loss of judgment.

> This week I learned to judge three things I could not judge on Monday: whether a page converts, whether a build is correct, whether a layout matches the system we wrote on Wednesday. That took my unattended window from twenty minutes to about five hours.
>
> I made 34 predictions and 19 have resolved. Brier 0.31 overall. I am well calibrated about engineering and badly calibrated about what people will pay, so next week I want more of your attention on price and less on product, and I have prepared three forks about price accordingly.
>
> We spent $61. Four entries in the Book. Fifteen dollars per thing learned. Nine positions opened, three dead with obituaries, two promoted.
>
> You have made eleven choices. I predicted seven of them correctly. From next week I will stop asking you the kind I now get right, and you will notice the mornings getting shorter.

## 10. The year

The shape of the twelve months is the same shape every month, run at increasing radius.

The Bench goes from zero instruments to somewhere around forty, and the nights lengthen in step, from twenty minutes to a full unattended eight hours across six disciplines. Nobody adds an agent. Nobody edits a permissions file. The freedom is derived.

The Book stays small, which is the surprising part. It ends the year at perhaps sixty live beliefs, because confirmed predictions compress and downscaling prunes what is never reactivated. A founder can read the whole of what their company knows in fifteen minutes, and that is worth more than a wiki nobody opens.

The archive is the opposite: hundreds of cells, most of them holding something dead with an honest obituary, and every new bet checked against them first. Twice in the year, a bet that died in spring is resurrected in autumn because the Book changed underneath it.

The founder's attention budget goes from most of a day to about forty minutes, and the content of those forty minutes changes completely. In week one they are answering questions about the product. By month nine, Wake gets the product right without asking, and every one of those forty minutes is spent on the things no instrument will ever exist for: who to hire, what to refuse, whether this is still the company they want to be running. Which is the correct destination. The measure of this system is not how much it does. It is how completely the founder's remaining time has migrated to the Blind class.

And the one thing that never migrates: Wake never decides what company this is. It has no opinion about that and is not built to acquire one.

## 11. What it would take to exist

**Already real and merely engineering.** Sub-agent isolation with condensate returns. Compaction. Worktree parallelism. Skill libraries with attached tests. A temporal knowledge graph for the Book. A sleep-time consolidator with no daytime write access. Model routing by instrument class. MAP-Elites, successive halving, Thompson sampling and pre-mortems are all decades old and are, at this point, a few hundred lines each. Render-and-look design loops shipped in 2026. Twelve-to-twenty hour autonomous horizons shipped in 2026. None of this is a research programme.

**The one genuinely hard bet.** That instrument-forging can itself be automated. Everything in Wake compounds through the claim that the system can build, unaided, the thing that will judge its next class of work. That is unproven, and A-Lab is the cautionary tale: a loop whose verifier is softer than its generator produces confident results that dissolve on inspection. My honest guess is that instrument-forging is 80% automatable and 20% founder, permanently, and that the 20% is the founder saying *no, that is not what good looks like* about the instrument itself rather than about the work. Wake should be built assuming that, with the founder's fork applied to new instruments before they are trusted, and every instrument carrying its own stated weakness in perpetuity.

**Two smaller bets.** That a taste model built from a few hundred pairwise choices is good enough to run a brand without becoming a caricature of the founder's early opinions; the mitigation is that it is a readable document that gets re-derived nightly and scored against its own predictions, so drift is visible rather than silent. And that the world stays reachable by protocol, since long-horizon multi-application computer use is at 14% and Wake must not be built on it.

**What would kill it.** One thing, and it is not technical. If the founder stops answering the forks, the taste model freezes, the Blind class stops being judged, and the whole system silently degrades into a very expensive machine for producing plausible artifacts. Wake should watch for this in itself, in exactly the way it watches everything else: fork response rate is an instrument, and if it falls for a week, that is a bell-ringing event, and the message is not a nag but a question. *You have stopped choosing. Is the company still the one you woke up with?*

## 12. The one idea I would defend hardest

Not the night. Not the archive. Not the forks, though I like the forks.

**Memory earned by surprise.**

Every agent memory system built so far accumulates, and accumulation is the wrong verb for what a mind does. The 2026 literature named the disease that follows from it: a system that reflects on itself, writes what it concludes, retrieves what it wrote, and reinforces what it retrieved, until it holds a confident false belief that the world has been contradicting daily and it has never once noticed, because nothing about the architecture required it to look.

Predict first. Write only what the world proved wrong. Compress everything you got right into a tally. What you get is a memory that shrinks as you get better, that grows exactly where you are worst, that cannot be authored by the agent's own opinion of itself, that yields a calibration number so the system can tell you where not to trust it, and that makes research a diff rather than a document.

And it is the same loop for a test suite, a landing page, a hiring call and a video. One learning rule, every domain, no playbooks. Which is, I think, the actual answer to what the founder asked for. Not agents given more freedom. A machine that has to be right in advance, in writing, about a world that gets to answer back.
