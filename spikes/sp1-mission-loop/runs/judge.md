## Assessment

**Where A wins.** Every spot check landed, and A's prospecting motion *is* its proof artifact: submit the firm's own web form, call its own after-hours line, hand over a timestamped record of its own failures. A solo operator with no portfolio, no tooling, and no credibility can execute that tomorrow — and it converts the credibility problem (A's self-named weakest link) into a self-verifying document. Its honesty section is the strongest thing in either deliverable: it states the offer has never been shown to a prospect, discloses that a third independent source failed verification and refuses to count it, admits Reddit was inaccessible to its tooling, and labels its own price anchors as vendor buyer's-guide material rather than transactions. That is an evidence audit, not a risk boilerplate.

**A's real gaps.** It swallowed the rankings.io $284/$468 pair without noticing the source contradicts itself, then anchored its break-even on it. More seriously, it says nothing about state-bar rules on non-lawyer intake, conflicts checking, or confidentiality — for a product that handles inbound legal calls and promises an SLA-backed refund, that omission is a live risk its risk section should have caught.

**Where B is better.** Its source base is more institutional — Census of Governments, IBISWorld, First Research — where A leans on AI-receptionist vendor blogs. The named live solicitations (Battle Creek, Moorpark) are good concrete texture, and "AI-drafted compliance claims must be human-verified against source RFP text" is the sharpest single risk either document raises.

**Why B places second.** Two of its claims are presented in quotation marks and I could not confirm either sentence, and one of them — the CA/TX/MI ranking — is what its entire first-month geographic focus stands on, cited to a page that rewrites itself daily. Its risk section audits the business but never audits its own evidence: no acknowledgment that the offer is untested on a prospect, no flag on which sources are weak. And it leaves a pricing tension unresolved: it invokes the 6.3% margin to argue $2,500 is unaffordable, then asks for $1,250, which is still a large bite out of the annual profit on a single small contract. Its 30-day path also front-loads real setup (build a monitoring workflow, compile 30–50 contractors) before first contact, and the buyer is only in-market when a relevant RFP happens to be open — a timing constraint on a 30-day close that B doesn't price in.

Both are genuine, reachable niches with defensible first offers. A is the one I'd bet 30 days on, mainly because day one costs nothing and produces a document the prospect cannot argue with.
